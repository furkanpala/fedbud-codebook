Lesson 6

Importance scores

Before it cuts, FedBUD measures how much each head and each neuron contributes on the client's own data.

The idea

Lesson 4 showed that some heads matter much more than others[1]. Trying every combination of parts is far too slow for a real model, so FedBUD gives every head and every neuron an importance score, a number that estimates how much the part contributes.

A head matters if it produces a large output and if the next layer's parameters read that output strongly. So the score of a head is the average size of its output over the client's tokens, times the size of the parameter values that read it. FedBUD builds the score of a feed-forward neuron the same way, from the average size of the neuron's output and the size of the parameter values that carry it on. FedBUD divides each score by the total of its layer, so the scores of one layer add up to one. It computes all scores in one pass of the large model over 32 of the client's training examples.

Because the scores come from the client's own data, two clients with different data can get different scores, and so different sub-networks under the same budget. When FedBUD halves a block, it keeps the heads or neurons with the highest scores.

Questions

Question 1

A layer has three heads with raw scores 2, 3 and 5. After FedBUD divides each score by the layer total, what is the score of the third head?

0.5. The layer total is 2 + 3 + 5 = 10, and 5 divided by 10 is 0.5.

Question 2

Why does the score of a head use both the size of its output and the size of the parameter values that read it?

The first answer. A part can only change the model's answer if it produces a large output and the next parameter values pass that output on. The product of the two sizes captures both.

Question 3

Compare the blue and the orange bars for the heads, and compare the two accuracies for the neurons. How well do the scores predict which parts matter?

Only in part. The scores often find the most and the least important heads, but they can swap two heads in the middle, and for the neurons of this toy layer they may do no better than a random choice. A score is a quick estimate from 32 examples and one pass of the model, while the accuracy drop needs a full test for every part. That is why FedBUD uses the scores only to pick which heads and neurons to keep inside a block. How much of each block to keep is a second choice, which FedBUD checks by training and testing candidates, as Lesson 7 shows.

Summary

    
FedBUD scores every head and neuron by how large its output is on the client's data and how strongly the next parameter values read it, and it divides the scores of a layer by their total. The scores come from one pass over 32 of the client's examples, so they are cheap, and they differ between clients with different data. Inside each block, the sub-network keeps the parts with the highest scores.
