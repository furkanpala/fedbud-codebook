Lesson 7

The allocator: choosing what each client keeps

The allocator chooses, for each client, how much of every block to keep within the client's budget.

The idea

Lesson 6 gave every part a score. The allocator now chooses the configuration, the action of every block, so that the sub-network fits the budget and still works well on the client's data. A simple way is a rule written by hand. The paper tests two such rules. One removes feed-forward neurons first, lowest score first, until the sub-network fits. The other narrows every block by the same share.

FedBUD learns the choice instead. Its allocator is a policy, a small network that reads the client's graph from Lesson 5 and gives every block a probability for each action[1]. In each of the 40 iterations of the search, the policy draws 8 configurations for the client. A sampled configuration can cost more than the budget, so a budget repair step makes it cheaper, block by block, starting where the policy is most willing to give up, until it fits. The policy then raises the probability of the configurations that did well, and lowers the others. Researchers call this kind of learning from trial and reward reinforcement learning[2].

To judge a configuration properly, FedBUD would train the cut model briefly and test it, which takes about a minute per candidate. Measuring the loss right after cutting takes about two seconds. So the search ranks candidates by the quick loss, corrects it with a small learned formula, and trains and tests only the best candidates. The client then gets the tested configuration with the lowest loss.

Questions

Question 1

The policy draws 8 configurations in each of 40 iterations. How many configurations does it draw for one client in a whole search?

320, which is 8 × 40. At about a minute each, training and testing all of them would take more than five hours per client. Ranking them by the quick loss of two seconds takes about ten minutes, which is why FedBUD trains and tests only the best ones.

Question 2

A sampled configuration costs 0.7 and the client's budget is 0.6. What does FedBUD do with it?

The second answer. The budget repair keeps every configuration within the budget, so the budget is a hard limit and not a wish.

Question 3

Look at your table. Does the learned policy always outperform the hand-designed rules? Does any way outperform random sub-networks?

Your toy table may differ from run to run. In the paper, against random sub-networks at the same budget, the sub-networks FedBUD selected scored higher on five of six datasets and equal on the sixth, so the choice of parts matters. Against the hand-designed rules, the better rule scored higher than the selected sub-networks on four of six datasets. So a simple rule on the same importance scores can replace the learned search, and the paper says so.

Summary

    
The allocator chooses, for each client, how much of every block to keep within the budget. FedBUD learns this choice with a policy that reads the client's graph, draws configurations, repairs them to the budget and rewards the good ones. It ranks candidates by a quick loss and trains and tests only the best. In the paper, its choices outperformed random sub-networks, while a simple rule on the same scores did as well or better on most datasets.
