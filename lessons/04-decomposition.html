Lesson 4

The decomposition idea

Keep for each client only the parts of the large model that fit its budget. The kept parts form the client's sub-network.

The idea

A language model is a stack of layers. Each layer has two kinds of parts. Attention heads let each token look at the other tokens of the text and collect what it needs from them. Feed-forward neurons then change each token on its own[1].

Every head and every neuron costs computation for each token. If a client drops some heads and some neurons, the remaining model is cheaper[2, 3, 4]. The remaining model is the client's sub-network. Cutting one large model into one sub-network per client is decomposition, the idea of this course. The hard part is to drop parts so that the sub-network still works well. The paper cuts in steps. For each attention block it keeps all or half of the heads, or drops the block. For each feed-forward block it keeps all, half or a quarter of the neurons, or drops the block. Inside a block it keeps the heads and neurons with the highest importance scores, which Lesson 6 explains.

Try it: cut a small transformer layer

Your browser trains a tiny model with one layer of 4 heads and 16 neurons. Its task is simple. It reads 8 tokens and answers whether the first token appears again later. The model needs its heads to compare the first token with the others. Switch parts off and watch the cost and the accuracy.

Questions

Question 1

In the toy layer, one head costs 256 multiplications per token and one neuron costs 32. The full layer has 4 heads and 16 neurons. What is the cost of a sub-network that keeps 2 heads and 8 neurons, as a fraction of the full layer?

0.5. The full layer costs 4 × 256 + 16 × 32 = 1,536. The sub-network costs 2 × 256 + 8 × 32 = 768, which is half.

Question 2

Switch off one head at a time and note the accuracy. Do all heads matter equally?

In most toy models, some heads matter much more than others. Dropping an important head costs a lot of accuracy, while dropping another costs almost nothing. So a good sub-network keeps the important parts. Lesson 6 shows how FedBUD measures how important each part is.

Question 3

Set the budget to 0.6. Find the most accurate sub-network you can within that budget. Which parts did you keep, and how far below the full layer is it?

The answer depends on the toy model your browser trained. A good sub-network keeps the heads that matter most and drops the parts that matter least for their cost. One head costs as much as eight neurons, so dropping a weak head frees a lot of budget. Finding the best choice by hand takes many tries even for one small layer. A real model has many layers, so FedBUD makes this choice with an algorithm, as Lesson 7 shows.

Summary

    
Decomposition keeps, for each client, only some heads and neurons of the large model, so that the client's sub-network fits its budget. Parts differ in cost and in how much they matter, so the choice of parts decides how well the sub-network works. FedBUD makes this choice per client, on the client's own data, as the next lessons show.
