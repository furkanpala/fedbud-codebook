Lesson 8

Training together with different sub-networks

When clients keep different parts, the server must average each part only over the clients that keep it, a rule the paper names aligned averaging.

The idea

FedBUD starts each client's sub-network with weight inheritance, where a new sub-network starts from the trained parameter values of the full model for the parts it keeps, instead of from random numbers. The client then trains a small update on its own data. In the paper this update is a LoRA update[1], which leaves the inherited parameters frozen and trains two small matrices of parameters for each matrix of the model.

Federated averaging from Lesson 2 averages the clients' copies position by position. With different sub-networks, this goes wrong. A client that keeps neurons 1, 2, 5 and 6 stores them in rows 1 to 4 of its matrices, while a client that keeps neurons 3, 4, 7 and 8 stores those in its rows 1 to 4. Averaging row 2 of both mixes neuron 2 with neuron 4. Aligned averaging labels every row with the head or neuron it belongs to, and averages each head or neuron only over the clients that keep it[2, 3, 4]. A part that only one client keeps keeps that client's values, and no client receives values for a part it does not have.

Questions

Question 1

Clients A and B keep neuron 5, and client C does not. A holds 100 examples and has the value 0.2 for one parameter of neuron 5. B holds 300 examples and has the value 0.6. What value does aligned averaging give?

0.5, which is (100 × 0.2 + 300 × 0.6) divided by 400. Client C does not count, because it does not keep neuron 5.

Question 2

Client A keeps all 8 neurons, client B keeps neurons 1, 2, 5 and 6, and client C keeps neurons 3, 4, 7 and 8. Position by position, the server averages row 2 of all three clients. Which neurons does it mix?

Neuron 2 of A and B with neuron 4 of C, because C stores neuron 4 in its second row. Client C then receives back an average that partly belongs to another neuron.

Question 3

In your run, when is the gap between the two ways of averaging largest, in the first rounds or at the end? Why?

In most toy runs the gap is largest in the first rounds. Right after the first average, a client that stores its neurons in other rows receives parameter values that belong partly to other neurons, so it loses part of what it inherited. Local training then repairs some of the damage, and the gap may shrink or even reverse after many rounds. Aligned averaging avoids the damage in the first place.

Question 4

What does aligned averaging do when every client keeps the same parts? And did it help in the paper?

When every client keeps the same parts, aligned averaging is plain federated averaging. In the paper, against averaging position by position, FedBUD scored higher on five of six datasets in one series of runs. On the federation where four clients hold four different tasks, it raised the loss in both series. With one run per setting, the paper could not establish an effect, so it calls this result partial support and advises testing aligned averaging first when clients hold different tasks.

Summary

    
Each sub-network inherits the trained parameter values of the parts it keeps and learns a small update on the client's data. Because clients keep different parts, the server labels every row with its head or neuron and averages each one only over the clients that keep it. When all clients keep the same parts, this is plain federated averaging. In the paper it scored higher than averaging position by position on most datasets, but not when clients held different tasks.
